# 8 Things Your Cross Country Coach Wants You to Know

*Advice for Ohio's cross country runners, gathered from coaches across the state.*

Your coach has about thirty seconds between drills to tell you what actually matters. Most of what they know about racing and training gets compressed into a line like "trust the process" or "run your own race," because there's rarely time to say more once the season is underway.

Here's the longer version -- eight things Ohio coaches wish they had more time to say.

## 1. Your Pre-Race Nerves Are Not a Problem to Solve

Stomach in knots. Can't eat breakfast. Replaying the start line three hours before the gun. If that's you, you're not broken -- you're normal.

Nerves are your body getting ready to do something hard. Athletes who feel nothing before a big race usually aren't the ones who run their best one. The goal was never to eliminate the nerves -- it's learning to run well *with* them. That comes from experience, not a trick you learn the night before.

## 2. Ten Minutes of Core Work Beats a Season in a Boot

Skipping strength and mobility work feels harmless -- it isn't mileage, so it doesn't feel like "real" training. But most season-ending injuries don't come from one bad workout. They come from small weaknesses that never got addressed: a hip that doesn't stabilize, a core that fatigues halfway through a race, an ankle with no range of motion.

Ten minutes of drills after practice isn't extra credit. It's often the difference between running healthy from August to November and watching the back half of the season from a boot.

## 3. Under-Eating Will End Your Season Faster Than Any Workout Builds It

Cross country culture has a bad habit of treating hunger like discipline. It isn't. Athletes who chronically under-fuel don't get leaner and faster -- they get injured, sick, and slower, often without connecting it back to what's on their plate.

Distance running burns a serious amount of energy. Eating enough, consistently, especially around hard workouts, isn't something to feel guilty about -- it's as much a part of the training plan as the workout itself.

## 4. Tell Your Coach the Moment Something Hurts -- Not After

There's a version of toughness that says you push through pain and don't complain. That version ends seasons. The athletes who stay healthy aren't the ones who never feel anything -- they're the ones who mention a tight calf on day one, before it becomes a stress fracture by day twenty.

Your coach isn't going to bench you for speaking up. They're going to adjust a few things and probably save you three weeks on the shelf. Hiding pain doesn't make you tougher. It just makes the injury longer.

## 5. Your Coach Notices the Days Results Don't Show

The scoreboard only tells part of the story. Coaches remember the practice in 38-degree rain when half the team found a reason to skip it and you didn't. They remember who asked for one more rep instead of hoping nobody counted.

Results are loud, but effort is what coaches actually track over a season. More often than not, it's effort -- not raw talent -- that decides who gets the varsity spot or the benefit of the doubt on a bad day.

## 6. A Good Race and a Fast Race Aren't Always the Same Race

Some of the most impressive races a coach watches all season never show up as a PR. A gutsy move with 800 meters left. Holding a pack together through a rough patch. Fighting for 15th when 15th is exactly what the team needed.

Time is one measure of a race. It's not the only one. Coaches are watching execution -- did you run the plan, respond to what happened, compete when it got hard -- because that's the skill that eventually turns into fast times anyway.

## 7. Your Freshman Ranking Doesn't Decide Your Senior Season

Plenty of state qualifiers were nowhere near the top of their own team as freshmen. Development in this sport is rarely a straight line -- it's closer to a series of jumps, plateaus, and occasional steps backward, spread across years, not weeks.

Coaches have watched this pattern enough times to trust it, even when an athlete can't see it yet. If progress feels slow right now, that's not the same as progress stopping.

## 8. This Sport Was Never Just About Running

Somewhere in the middle of a brutal tempo run or a summer of 6 a.m. mileage, cross country starts teaching things that have nothing to do with pace. Showing up when you don't feel like it. Finishing what you start. Handling a bad day without letting it define the season.

That's the part coaches actually care about most -- not the times, not the medals. If this sport does its job, you leave it more disciplined and more resilient than you found it. Everything else is a bonus.
